Jev, explained simply

What it is, how it was built, whether the numbers hold up, what people built with it, and — new in this version — what outside probes and Mac mini measurements say about the open-source clones. Written for someone who has not been following the launch. Clone numbers and source labels from research-claw, 2026-09-21.

Start here: the one-paragraph version

An ordinary AI model writes you an answer, word by word, and you read it. Jev does not write anything. You hand it a form you designed — a list of questions, each with the answers it is allowed to give — and it fills the form in. Next to every answer it puts a number: how sure it is. That is the entire product.

Because it never writes a sentence, it cannot ramble, cannot invent a citation, and cannot hand you text your program has to parse. It answers the questions you wrote, in the shape you asked for, and it does it in about a tenth of a second.

The form is the product. Everything below follows from this one choice.

1. How it is built

The name: System One

Psychologists split thinking into two modes. System 1 is fast and automatic — you see a face and know instantly that it is angry. System 2 is slow and deliberate — you multiply 17 × 24 on paper. Chat models are built for System 2 work: think it through, show the reasoning, write it out. TypeSafe built Jev for the System 1 half: the snap judgment, made a thousand times a second, that no human was ever going to read.

The training: RLCD

Chat models are trained with RLHF — humans rate answers, the model learns to produce answers humans rate highly. Jev is trained with RLCD, Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions. The target is different: the model is rewarded for putting honest numbers on its answers. If it says 0.85 (the confidence), answers in that bucket should be right about 85% of the time — that is the confidence score, not a claim that this one answer is correct. TypeSafe's phrase is "epistemically honest probabilities," and their stated guarantee is that higher confidence means higher accuracy.

That is the whole bet. A confidence number you can trust is what lets a program decide on its own — send this one straight through, send that one to a human — without a person reading every case.

The speed trick: answer everything at once

A chat model writes one token, looks at what it wrote, then writes the next. A hundred tokens means a hundred trips. Jev does a single pass and all the answers come out together. Asking a tenth question costs a few more input tokens and almost no extra time, which inverts the usual instinct to keep calls small.

Giving up sentence-writing is what buys the flat latency. It is a trade, not a free lunch.

TypeSafe's headline is 193.6× faster and 444.6× cheaper, and to their credit they label that the high end of real-world gains rather than the average. They also publish their own caveats, which is unusually honest for a launch: shorter inputs flatter their model, the comparison workflows were written by their own team, and they ran no standard public benchmark at all — the evidence is custom "workflow evals."

Then 12,759 people tried it in public over four days and posted their own measurements. Those are lower.

From 215 user-reported speed figures and 180 cost figures. Neither bar is a benchmark: one side chose its own baseline, the other is an average over hundreds of different baselines.

Do not promote 7× to the new true number. It is better evidence than the vendor's figure and it is still not a benchmark. Two things are wrong with it: people who got an interesting result posted it and people whose test was boring or broken did not, so the sample is self-selected; and every person picked their own comparison model and their own task, so 7× is an average over an unknown mix of baselines.

The third row is the one that would settle it, and it is empty.

One number in the pile does survive: the 76 ms median latency. An absolute measurement does not depend on what anyone compared against, so self-selection bruises it far less than it bruises a ratio. It also sits at the fast end of TypeSafe's own 70–500 ms range, which is the rare case of both sides agreeing. Take the speed as real and treat every multiplier as a claim about someone's baseline.

The claim doing the most work — that a 0.85 really means 85% — has not been independently verified by anyone. It came up repeatedly in the launch discussion and remains open. The last section says how you would check it.

The one that took off on its own was context pruning: an AI agent accumulates a huge history, and Jev scores each old step for whether it still matters, so the expensive model only re-reads what counts. That thread drew 2.1M views in two days. The main objection, from @theo, is that pruning throws away context whose value you only discover later.

The sharpest demonstration of the boundary came from a developer who generated text with Jev anyway — one character at a time, using 29 yes/no questions per character. It works, and it is a very expensive way to do what any chat model does for free. It shows exactly where the edge of the tool is.

What the critics said

Roughly 4% of posts were explicitly negative, but about 1,376 carried a concrete objection. The recurring ones: that this may be constrained decoding on a small model rather than a new architecture; that encoder-only classifiers have done this for years, so "really smart switch statement" is the fair baseline; that the speed comparisons used different baselines on different tasks; and that nobody has checked the calibration. Access was the loudest complaint by far — 507 mentions of the waitlist against 13 about price.

4. What's known, inferred, and undisclosed

Sources for this section: TypeSafe docs, Archer Hume's black-box probe ("Jev's Architecture Unmasked"), and TypeSafe's own jaggedness / FAQ pages. "Known" means TypeSafe or a public probe stated it; "inferred" is the strongest reading of those probes; "undisclosed" is still blank on purpose.

Known

  • Starts as a pretrained LM; post-training is RLCD (Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions) — TypeSafe's third branch after RLHF / RLVR (docs.typesafe.ai/introduction/machine-learning-primer).
  • One request = one state (up to 64k tokens; 32k for state plus the longest single question) + typed questions answered in parallel, in isolation.
  • Question types: Choice (≤255 options), Score, Noul. Output tokens are free; jev-1.13.0 is $0.042 per million input tokens (docs.typesafe.ai/models).
  • Typical hosted latency in our hands: 0.25–0.45 s per request over the network.
  • Probe: a secret planted in a sibling question gets 0.00 probability; the same secret in shared state gets 0.90–0.92.
  • Probe: state is encoded once — token accounting is strictly additive (one question 268 tokens, two 276).
  • Probe: latency is the same for 2 options or 255; output_tokens does not move with answer values — probabilities are read out directly.
  • Probe: adding an irrelevant option shifts odds between existing ones (list-wise scoring, not independent per-option logits).
  • Calibration on one public set: ECE 0.031 over 1,200 MMLU items.
  • Jaggedness (TypeSafe): reads instructions literally; cannot count or do date arithmetic; degrades with irrelevant state text; can be steered by adversarial content in state; English-first; not fine-tuned per customer.

Inferred

  • Backbone is almost certainly a causal transformer: 84.6% on MMLU-Pro needs frontier-scale pretraining, and RLCD is described as post-training.
  • Diogo Almeida co-invented RLHF — the family resemblance (LM + new post-training objective) is not an accident.

Undisclosed

  • Base model (tokenizer matched none of 192 public tokenizers), dense vs. MoE, exact loss, whether RLCD updates the whole network or just the head.
  • Launch FAQ leaves "training algorithm", "training data", and "public benchmarks" blank — not even a simple RAG retrieval comparison against chat LLMs or classical ML on a shared public set.

5. The open-source clones

Six open-source "clones" appeared within 48 hours of launch. All of them copy the interface (typed questions in, probabilities out) using the recipe Hume reverse-engineered: take an open LLM, pack state and options into one sequence, put a small head on the last position, train with plain cross-entropy on labelled answers.

Takeaways

  1. Clones were built for a different job. Their benches are short-text classification. Browser and phone agents need long state + many options in one pass (64k tokens, 255 options, parallel questions). No clone has published a number on that shape; the first time we tried, all failed on quality, not just speed.
  2. They are not calibrated — it shows when stopping. On the phone task kev and Nimble navigate, then never say "done". Jev stops at 0.77–0.81 confidence. Nimble's README: a 0.9 does not mean 90%.
  3. Hardware explains speed, not mistakes. M4/16 GB is 3–10× slower than authors' M5/32 GB or H100 machines (why kev-4b thrashed on 4k tokens). It does not explain wrong tickets, BLOCKED-before-looking, or never saying "done".
  4. What the $40M and secret data bought: general zero-shot behaviour, honest probabilities, and long context — from training data and objective, not from the architecture trick the community reproduced in two days.

Practical guidance

  • Fixed labels, short text, own labelled data: kev-0.5b/4b or Nimble locally is a real option — same answers as Jev, no API bill. Add post-hoc calibration before trusting probabilities.
  • Anything agentic (browser, phone, long documents, 20+ options): use Jev. Today nothing open gets close.
  • If you build on Jev: the model is rarely the bottleneck. Our browser loop spent 90% of time in the DOM reader and text helper; the phone loop spent 90% in the simulator controller.
  • The one test nobody has run yet: calibration on your data — bucket confidences on a few thousand of your own decisions and check whether the 0.8 bucket is right 80% of the time.

7. Can you make it better? Prompt engineering, context, and self-improvement

The model never learns from you. Every improvement lives in your code. There is no fine-tuning, no feedback loop, and no self-improvement published — the weights you call today are the weights you call in six months. That moves the whole question from "how do I train this" to "how do I wire this up," which is a different job with different people doing it.

Within that, there is a real craft, and it splits in two: how you write the questions, and what you do with the numbers that come back.

Writing the questions (this is the prompt engineering)

  • It reads you literally. It answers the question you typed, not the one you meant. Negations and scope words land at face value. If you find yourself explaining what you really meant, rewrite the question.
  • Break big judgments into small ones. Instead of "is this a good candidate," ask separately about Python depth, leadership, and system design, each on its own scale.
  • Retrieve first, then judge. Accuracy drops when you pad the state with irrelevant material. Fetch precisely in code, send only the fields the question needs. TypeSafe says this improves calibration too.
  • Give it an escape hatch. Include "other" or "not stated" as an option, or you force a confident answer out of ambiguous material.

The improvement loop

Because the model is frozen, tuning moves to the weights you apply to its scores — which you can version and A/B test like any other code.

The other lever is confidence-gated routing: set a different bar per action, scaled to what being wrong costs. A read-only action can act on 0.7; anything that moves money waits for more, or goes to a human. This only works if the confidence number is honest, which is why calibration is the claim that matters.

Things it simply cannot do

  • Write anything — no text, no code, no summaries.
  • Arithmetic, counting, or date comparisons. Extract the pieces as choices and do the maths in code.
  • Images, for now. The Doom demo fed it a text description of the game state, not pixels.
  • More than 255 options in one Choice.
  • Resist context contamination: text written by a user can sit in the state and argue for its own classification. Treat it as untrusted input.

One habit worth stealing

You can pin the exact version — TypeSafeClient(model="jev-1.13.0") — and every response says which version answered it. That sounds minor and is not. A model that changes underneath you silently makes every accuracy number you measured quietly expire, with no notice. Being able to pin a version and see it in the response belongs on the evaluation checklist next to latency and price, and it is absent from every vendor's comparison page.

How you would actually check the main claim

Everything rests on the confidence number being honest, and no one outside TypeSafe has tested it. The test is not complicated: run a few thousand decisions where you already know the right answer, bucket them by the confidence returned, and see whether the 0.8 bucket really is right about 80% of the time. Plot that, and the gap between the curve and the diagonal is your answer — that gap has standard names, expected calibration error and Brier score.

What makes it awkward is not the method but the data: you need thousands of cases with known correct answers, in a domain you care about. Anyone holding a labelled corpus can settle publicly, in an afternoon, the one question the launch left open.
